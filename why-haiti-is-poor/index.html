Why is Haiti so poor?

The first free Black republic was billed for its own freedom. The invoice took 122 years to clear.

Last updated August 2026

Why is Haiti so poor?Haiti is poor primarily because of its history, not its people. After winning independence in 1804, Haiti was forced to pay France a crushing indemnity for its own freedom — and the debts that grew from it weren’t cleared until 1947. A US occupation, colonial-era environmental damage, and ongoing political instability have compounded that foundation ever since.

Haiti’s history, in nine dates

  • 1492

    Columbus lands on Hispaniola. Within decades, the indigenous Taíno population is nearly destroyed by disease and forced labor.

  • 1697–1791

    As French Saint-Domingue, Haiti becomes the most profitable colony in the world[1] — sugar and coffee produced by roughly 500,000 enslaved Africans[12], worked so brutally that deaths outpaced births and the colony imported tens of thousands of people a year to replace them.

  • 1804

    After a thirteen-year revolution, Haiti declares independence: the first nation founded by formerly enslaved people.

  • 1825

    French warships arrive. King Charles X demands 150 million francs as “compensation” to former slaveholders. Haiti, under threat of invasion, agrees — and borrows from French banks to pay, creating the “double debt.”[1, 3]

  • 1838

    The indemnity is reduced to 90 million francs; payments and the loans stacked on top of them continue for the rest of the century.[3]

  • 1915–1934

    The United States occupies Haiti for nineteen years, seizing control of its customs and finances.

  • 1947

    Haiti makes its final payment on the loans descended from the ransom — to National City Bank of New York — 122 years after the warships appeared.[1, 5]

  • 2010

    A magnitude-7.0 earthquake devastates Port-au-Prince. The death toll remains disputed: from a US-commissioned study’s 46,000–85,000 to the Haitian government’s official 316,000.[13]

  • 2021

    President Jovenel Moïse is assassinated in July; in August a magnitude-7.2 earthquake kills about 2,200 people in the south.[14]

Did Haiti really pay France for its independence?

Yes — and this single fact explains more about Haiti’s poverty than any other.

In 1825, twenty-one years after Haitians defeated Napoleon’s army and declared independence, French warships appeared off Port-au-Prince. King Charles X’s demand: 150 million francs as compensation to former slaveholders for their “lost property.” That property was the Haitian people themselves. The full demand amounted to roughly ten times Haiti’s annual revenue (Bibliothèque nationale de France)[2] — and The New York Times found that the first installment alone was about six times the government’s income that year (NYT, citing 19th-century Haitian historian Beaubrun Ardouin)[1].

Facing invasion and a return to slavery, Haiti agreed. And because it could not pay, it borrowed — from the very French banks connected to the deal. Historians call this the double debt: Haiti paid interest to French lenders on money it borrowed to pay the French state.

The New York Times’ 2022 investigation, The Ransom, tracked every payment and calculated that Haiti paid the equivalent of about $560 million in today’s dollars to satisfy the ransom and the loans it was forced to take to pay it. Had that money simply stayed in Haiti’s economy and grown at the country’s own pace, the Times estimates it would have added $21 billion over time — and using neighboring countries’ growth rates, as much as $115 billion, eight times the size of Haiti’s entire economy in 2020.[1]

The double debt itself was finally cleared in the 1880s — but the borrowing it forced never stopped. By 1922, National City Bank of New York (the predecessor of Citibank) controlled nearly all of Haiti’s foreign debt, and Haiti’s final payment on the loans descended from the ransom wasn’t made until 1947.[1, 5] For 122 years, money that could have built schools, roads, and hospitals sailed to Paris and New York instead.

“It became the world’s first and only country where the descendants of enslaved people paid reparations to the descendants of their masters — for generations.”

— The New York Times, “The Ransom” (2022)

It’s also why we obsess over where every donated dollar goes today.

How did colonization ruin Haiti’s land?

Before there was a debt, there was the soil.

French Saint-Domingue ran on sugarcane and coffee — monocrops that demanded cleared land. Colonial planters stripped forests from the hillsides, and after independence, debt pressure pushed Haiti to keep exporting timber and cash crops to make payments. The erosion that followed still bleeds Haiti’s topsoil into the sea with every heavy rain.

An honest note on a famous statistic: you may have read that Haiti has “less than 2% forest cover.” That figure is disputed — satellite studies put total tree cover closer to 20–30% depending on how forest is defined (Pauleus & Aide, PeerJ, 2020)[15]. What is not disputed is the damage: Haiti’s intact primary forest is under 1% (Hedges et al., PNAS, 2018)[16], and degraded farmland grows less food, which deepens rural poverty, which drives families to cut remaining trees for charcoal — the fuel most can afford. A colonial-era decision about sugar profits became a modern crisis of food security, and it’s why our work includes clean water projects rather than handouts alone.

What did the US occupation do to Haiti?

From 1915 to 1934, the United States occupied Haiti — nineteen years of foreign control justified as “stabilization.” The occupation actually announced itself earlier: in December 1914, US Marines removed $500,000 in gold from Haiti’s national bank and shipped it aboard the USS Machias to the New York vaults of National City Bank (US State Dept., Foreign Relations of the United States, 1914)[6].

During the occupation, American officials ran Haiti’s customs houses and treasury, directed Haitian finances toward repaying American and French banks, and imposed the 1918 constitution that allowed foreigners to own Haitian land for the first time since 1804[17]. US financial control formally ended only in 1947, with the last debt payment. The occupation left a template that outlasted it: Haitian sovereignty treated as negotiable, and Haitian institutions built to serve external creditors before Haitian citizens.

Why is Haiti so much poorer than the Dominican Republic?

Same island, two very different stories — and the border between them is how many people first notice Haiti’s poverty.

Part of the answer is everything above: the Dominican Republic never paid an independence ransom, inherited a less extractive colonial economy, and never had its treasury run from New York. But historians caution against single-cause stories — the divergence also reflects the twentieth century: different institutions, different export economies (Dominican sugar, then tourism and free-trade zones), and starkly different treatment by the United States and international lenders.[8]

The gap today: the Dominican Republic’s GDP per capita is roughly five times Haiti’s — $10,876 versus $2,143 in 2024 (World Bank)[8] — and by purchasing-power measures about seven times higher (Our World in Data)[9]. In 1990 Dominicans were only about twice as rich; the gap is not ancient, and it is still widening.

Geography gets blamed — hurricanes and earthquakes strike both nations — but disasters devastate Haiti more because poverty made it fragile first. The earthquake didn’t make Haiti poor; poverty made the earthquake catastrophic. See what the 2010 and 2021 earthquakes did.

Why hasn’t foreign aid fixed Haiti?

Because most of it never actually reached Haitians.

After the 2010 earthquake, donors allocated $13.34 billion for Haiti’s relief and recovery over 2010–2020 (UN Office of the Special Envoy for Haiti)[10]. But the overwhelming majority flowed through foreign contractors and international agencies. The Center for Economic and Policy Research found that of the billions disbursed in the first years, less than 0.6% went directly to Haitian organizations, institutions, or companies — and of USAID’s spending, “for every $1 that USAID has spent, less than one penny went directly to Haitian organizations… more than 50 cents went to Beltway firms” in and around Washington, DC (CEPR)[11]. Haiti earned a bitter nickname: the Republic of NGOs.

This isn’t an argument against helping Haiti. It’s an argument for helping differently: smaller, closer, and accountable to the people served. It’s the reason we work in one community we know by name — Pelerin — and the reason 100% of donations reach our programs instead of an office in Washington.

What’s keeping Haiti poor today?

The historical weights above set the stage; the current crisis keeps the pressure on. Haiti has had no elected national government since President Jovenel Moïse’s assassination on July 7, 2021 — and no national elections since 2016. A Transitional Presidential Council governed from 2024 until its mandate expired in February 2026, succeeded by an interim government. The first general elections in a decade were scheduled for August 30, 2026, though Haiti’s electoral council had warned that holding them on schedule might prove impossible.[18]

Meanwhile, armed groups control much of the capital, choking the ports and roads that food, fuel, and medicine travel. The human numbers, as of the most recent reporting rounds:

  • More than half of Haitians — about 5.8 million people (52%) — face acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3+), including over 1.8 million in emergency conditions. (IPC/CNSA analysis, Sept 2025–Feb 2026)[19]
  • About 1.45 million people are internally displaced. (IOM Displacement Tracking Matrix, Round 12, Dec 2025)[20]
  • Schools and hospitals in affected areas have closed or operate in fragments.

And yet — daily life continues. Markets open. Churches fill. Children in uniforms walk to the schools that remain, including the classrooms we support in Pelerin. Haiti’s poverty is a condition, not an identity. Here is our honest answer to “Is Haiti safe?”

A nation misunderstood

Among the many challenges Haiti faces, perhaps the most profound is being misunderstood. The nuances of its history, the resilience of its people, and the potential of its future are routinely flattened into simplified narratives. Understanding Haiti means recognizing that the world helped write its problems — and that solidarity, not sympathy, is what’s owed.

The history is written. Tomorrow’s pages are blank.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Haiti the poorest country in the Western Hemisphere?

Mainly history: Haiti paid France a crushing indemnity for its independence — debts tied to it lasted until 1947 — endured a nineteen-year US occupation, and inherited degraded farmland from colonial plantation agriculture. Political instability and disasters since have compounded, not created, that foundation.

Did Haiti pay France for its independence?

Yes. In 1825, under threat of invasion, Haiti agreed to pay 150 million francs (reduced to 90 million in 1838) as “compensation” to former slaveholders. Haiti borrowed heavily to pay; the loans descended from that ransom weren’t fully retired until 1947.

Has France ever repaid Haiti?

No. In May 2015, President Hollande spoke of settling France’s debt to Haiti — his office clarified within hours he meant only a “moral” debt. In April 2025, President Macron called the indemnity unjust and announced a joint historical commission, but no restitution has been paid.

Why is Haiti poorer than the Dominican Republic?

The Dominican Republic never paid an independence ransom, had a less extractive colonial economy, and built stronger institutions and export industries in the twentieth century. Today its GDP per capita is about five times Haiti’s ($10,876 vs. $2,143, World Bank 2024).

Is Haiti poor because of the 2010 earthquake?

No — the earthquake was catastrophic because Haiti was already poor. Fragile construction and thin institutions turned a natural disaster into a national one. Even the death toll is disputed, with estimates from 46,000–85,000 up to the official 316,000.

Why didn’t billions in aid fix Haiti?

Donors allocated $13.34 billion after the 2010 earthquake, but CEPR found less than 0.6% of early disbursements went directly to Haitian organizations — and less than a penny of every USAID dollar. Most flowed to foreign contractors instead.
